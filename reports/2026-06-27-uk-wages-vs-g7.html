UK wages, the long view: stalled at home, lapped by the US

Since the 2008 financial crisis, the average British wage has barely moved in real terms — up about 4% over fourteen years, while America’s rose four times as fast. The result is a typical pay packet still hovering around its 2008 level, and a widening gap with most of the G7.

What the data shows

For a decade the UK led the US on pay growth. Then it stopped.

The cleanest way to compare wages across borders is the OECD’s measure of average annual pay, converted to US dollars at purchasing-power parity and held in constant 2024 prices — so the figures strip out both exchange rates and inflation.1 Indexed to the year 2000, the British line rose briskly through the 2000s, outpacing the United States. After the 2008 crisis it flattened, while America’s climbed away.

One reading is that Britain’s pay problem is not a long, slow decline but a sharp, datable break: strong growth before the crisis, near-stagnation since.5

Key finding. On this measure UK real wages grew +16.4% in 2000–2010 — faster than the US (+10.0%) — then just +4.0% in 2010–2024, while the US added +16.1%. The British weakness is specifically post-crisis.1
In context

Where the UK sits in the G7

In 2024 the average British wage was about $63,700 a year on this PPP-adjusted measure — fourth of the seven G7 economies, below the United States, Germany and Canada, and above France, Italy and Japan.1 The gap with America is the striking one: a US wage of $82,933 is roughly 30% higher, a difference of about $19,200 a year.1

Since the crisis

Fourteen years, four per cent

Isolating the years since 2010 sharpens the picture. American real wages rose about 16%; German pay 14% and Canadian 11%. British wages added just 4.0% over the whole stretch — ahead of only Japan, which was flat, and Italy, where real pay actually fell.1 France, often cited as a comparator, edged ahead of the UK (+4.7%).

This is what “sluggish” looks like in the data: not a collapse, but a near-standstill that compounds. Britain has slid down the international growth tables — one academic analysis of 21 OECD economies ranks UK real-wage growth 16th over 2008–2022, against 5th and 6th in the two decades before.5

Why it happened

A productivity problem, mostly

Economists generally tie stagnant pay to stagnant productivity: real wages can only rise sustainably if output per hour does, and UK productivity growth has been unusually weak since 2008.5 Layered on top are the post-crisis squeeze itself — real earnings fell by roughly 7% between 2008 and 2014 as inflation outran pay4 — and a second, quieter phase of feeble growth that followed. The Resolution Foundation, a living-standards think tank, estimates that had pre-2008 trends simply continued, the average pay packet would be many thousands of pounds a year larger; that figure is a counterfactual, not a measurement, and is treated as such here.2

What the desk can say plainly: the recovery, when it came, was late. By April 2025 typical full-time weekly pay was £767, still about 2% below its real 2008 level on the ONS’s long-running survey — and only the very youngest workers, lifted by the minimum wage, were clearly ahead of where their counterparts stood in 2008.3 Real pay has been growing again recently, but slowly: about 0.2% above inflation in the latest ONS reading.6

What we don't know

The OECD figure is an economy-wide mean per full-time-equivalent employee, built from the national-accounts wage bill. It can be lifted by high earners — more so in the United States, where pay is more unequal — so the cross-country gap overstates how much better off the typical American worker is than the typical Briton; US median wage growth has lagged its mean. PPP conversion also moves the rankings, and these are reference-year snapshots, not the full annual path. Causes are debated and not settled here; the figures measure what happened, not conclusively why.

Where it's contested

Is Britain uniquely bad, or just in the slow lane?

The core facts above are not seriously disputed. What credible analysts disagree on is how exceptional the UK’s record really is.

A British outlier

The scale and persistence of the stagnation is unusual: the UK fell from the top third to the middle of OECD wage-growth rankings after 2008, and a continued pre-crisis trend would have left the average packet thousands of pounds higher — a break some describe as almost without modern precedent.25

Empirical dispute
A shared slowdown

Much of the rich world slowed after 2008. On the same OECD measure France grew only marginally faster than the UK since 2010 (+4.7% vs +4.0%), while Italy and Japan did worse — so “uniquely bad” overstates it. The UK is in the laggard cluster, not alone in it.1

Caveat. The single biggest wrinkle is mean versus median. The OECD series is an economy-wide average; the “typical” worker’s experience is better read from the median, and the verdict on whether UK pay has cleared its 2008 level shifts with both the pay measure (mean AWE vs median ASHE) and the inflation gauge (CPIH, CPI or RPI). On the median ONS survey, typical full-time pay only drew level with 2008 in the mid-2020s.3
