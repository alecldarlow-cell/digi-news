Money & well-being · what the data shows

Money keeps buying happiness — just less of it with every step

For most people, both day-to-day mood and overall life satisfaction keep climbing with income, and no study has yet found a ceiling. But the gains run through the logarithm of income, so each extra pound counts for less than the last. And once you retire, how dependable the money is starts to matter more than how much of it there is.

Two questions hiding in one

“Does money buy happiness?” is really two questions

Researchers split well-being into two things that move differently. Emotional well-being is how you feel during the moments of your life — the frequency of joy, stress or sadness yesterday. Life evaluation is the verdict you reach when you stop and rate your life as a whole, usually on a 0–10 ladder.3 Money touches the two unequally, and most public confusion comes from treating them as one.

On the second measure there is little argument: richer people, and richer countries, report clearly higher life evaluations, and the relationship shows no agreed ceiling.48 The fight has always been about the first measure — everyday mood — and whether it stops responding to money past some point.

What nearly every study agrees on is the shape of the link: well-being rises with the logarithm of income, not with income itself.21 That single fact is the whole story of “diminishing returns,” and it is worth seeing plainly. Under a log relationship, equal multiples of income deliver equal steps of well-being: going from $30,000 to $60,000 buys about the same lift as going from $300,000 to $600,000. Put the other way round, a fixed cash raise is worth steadily less the higher you start.

Key finding. Money does not stop mattering — but because well-being tracks the logarithm of income, the marginal pound buys less and less. The poorer you are, the more each pound moves the needle.
The threshold debate

The famous “$75,000” was never the whole picture

In 2010 Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton analysed more than 450,000 daily US surveys and reported that emotional well-being rose with income but flattened beyond about $75,000 a year, while life evaluation kept climbing.3 The $75,000 figure was only the midpoint of a $60,000–$90,000 band, but it lodged in the culture as a hard ceiling on happiness.1

A decade later Matthew Killingsworth pinged 33,391 Americans on their phones for 1,725,994 real-time mood reports and found no plateau at all: experienced well-being kept rising with log income, just as steeply above $80,000 as below.2 Rather than let the contradiction stand, the two ran an adversarial collaboration — a joint reanalysis refereed by a third researcher, Barbara Mellers.1

The resolution, published in 2023, is more interesting than either original. For roughly four in five people — the “happy majority” — happiness keeps rising with income with no ceiling, and for the happiest group it even accelerates. The flattening is real, but only for the least-happy fifth, whose mood stops improving at around $100,000 (the inflation-adjusted echo of 2010’s $75,000).1 As the authors put it, beyond that point the miseries that remain are not alleviated by high income.1 Money, in other words, lifts mood efficiently for people whose unhappiness is the kind money can fix; it does little for suffering rooted elsewhere.

Cause, or just correlation?

Does the money cause the happiness?

Almost all of the evidence above is correlational: happier, healthier people also tend to earn more, so the arrow could point either way. The cleanest test comes from lotteries, where wealth lands at random. Tracking thousands of Swedish winners 5–22 years on, Lindqvist, Östling and Cesarini found that an after-tax windfall of about $100,000 raised life satisfaction by 0.037 of a standard deviation — a lasting lift that showed no sign of fading.5 This is rare causal confirmation that money genuinely improves how people rate their lives.

But the same study found the effect on happiness and mental health — the in-the-moment, affective side — was smaller and statistically indistinguishable from zero.5 The split mirrors the two-question divide: a windfall reliably improves the verdict you reach about your life, while doing far less for your day-to-day mood. Money buys the ledger more than the mood.

After work

What changes when you retire

The shift shows up first as a change of slope. Following the same households for up to a decade in Britain — part of a three-country study by Felix Cheung and Richard Lucas — researchers found that the strength of the link between income and life satisfaction is not fixed across a lifetime: it is strongest in mid-career and weakens markedly once people pass retirement age.13 Set on the same 1–7 satisfaction scale, the working-life gradient is about two and a half times as steep as the retirement one for the same proportional rise in income.

In working life, the size of the pay cheque is the dominant financial input to well-being. In retirement the question quietly changes — from how much to will it last — and the data change with it. Analysing the US Health and Retirement Study, Keith Bender and Natalia Jivan found that income and wealth do raise retirement satisfaction, but the effect is small: each extra $1,000 of income, or $10,000 of wealth, shifts the chance of being in the happiest group by under one percentage point.6

What dominates instead is the nature of the money and the exit. A guaranteed, defined-benefit pension — an income for life — raises the odds of top-tier satisfaction far more than its cash value alone would predict, because it removes the central fear of later life: running out.67 And the single largest factor is whether retirement was chosen. Being forced to retire cuts the chance of being in the happiest group by about 30 points; poor health cuts it by roughly 20.6

This is not to say retirees are unhappy — the opposite. In the same data, about 60% call their retirement very satisfying and only 7.5% not at all,6 consistent with the long-noticed tendency for well-being to climb again in later life after a mid-life dip.6 A more recent industry survey by the Happiness Research Institute for Legal & General — an annuity provider, so read with that interest in mind — likewise reports a “happiness advantage” for retirees with guaranteed income, even after controlling for income level.9 The harder structural fact sits underneath: guaranteed income is getting rarer, with only about a third of current US retirees holding any pension income.10

Beyond the pay cheque

What moves happiness more than money does

Income is one input among several, and rarely the strongest. The annual World Happiness Report explains most of the gap between countries’ life evaluations with six factors: income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and freedom from corruption.10 The report is careful that these are correlations, not clean causal weights — but the social factors do as much work as the economic ones, and explain everyday emotions better than GDP does.10

The most direct evidence on what lasts comes from the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed the same people for more than 85 years. Its clearest finding is not about money at all: the quality of close relationships in mid-life was the single best predictor of who stayed happy and healthy into old age — a better predictor of late-life health than cholesterol.11 Researchers there put the health cost of chronic loneliness on a par with smoking.11

None of this makes money irrelevant — poverty is corrosive, and the steepest well-being gains are at the bottom of the income scale, exactly where the logarithm predicts. The honest summary is narrower: money reliably buys a floor and lifts the verdict you reach about your life, with sharply falling returns; the texture of daily happiness depends more on health, autonomy and the people around you.

What we don't know

Causation is only partly pinned down: outside the lottery studies, the income–happiness link is correlational, and reverse causation (happier people earn more) is plausible. The “$100,000” threshold for the unhappy minority was imported from the 2010 study, not estimated from the data, and at least one reanalysis argues a data-driven threshold could sit nearer $200,000 — or that the flattening is a statistical artefact.1 The retirement figures rest on US data from 2000 and are associations, not experiments. And almost all of the experience-sampling evidence is from wealthy, mostly Western populations, so the dollar thresholds travel poorly.4

Where it's genuinely contested

Does a whole country get happier as it gets richer?

Within a country at one moment, richer people are clearly happier. Whether national growth raises national happiness over decades is the long-running “Easterlin paradox,” and credible economists still disagree. It is an empirical dispute — the kind better data can, in principle, settle.

The paradox holds

Richard Easterlin argues that over the long run, as entire societies grow richer, average happiness does not durably rise: people adapt, and judge their incomes against shifting social reference points rather than in absolute terms.12

Empirical dispute
The paradox dissolves

Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers, using broader and longer data, find a clear, sustained link between GDP per head and well-being — across countries and over time, with no satiation point. They argue the paradox reflects thin, dated samples.8

Threads we left out

  • Relative income and status. How much of money’s effect is keeping up with the neighbours rather than absolute spending power — a live question inside the Easterlin debate.
  • How you spend it. Evidence that buying time, experiences and giving to others yields more happiness per pound than buying things.
  • The young and the U-curve. Recent signs that the long-standing mid-life dip in well-being is changing shape, with younger adults reporting falling happiness.
  • Country and culture. Why dollar thresholds differ sharply across regions, and what the Nordic countries’ persistent lead actually reflects.
