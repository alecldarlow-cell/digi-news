London's escape to the country has gone into reverse

Londoners bought 57,660 homes outside the capital in 2025 — down 44% from the 2021 pandemic peak of 103,310, and slightly below the number bought each year, on average, before Covid. Hybrid work hasn't disappeared, but house prices outside London have risen three times faster than in the capital, and employers are asking for more office days than they did in 2022. Both are pulling movers back.

What the data shows

The "race for space" has fully unwound — and then some

During the pandemic, tens of thousands of Londoners moved out of the capital in search of gardens, spare rooms and a plausible reason to never take the Northern line again. Analysis of Land Registry transaction data by estate agent Hamptons found that Londoners bought 103,310 homes outside the M25 in 2021, the peak year, financed in part by a wave of sales in the capital worth £54.9bn.1

That peak has not just passed — it has been undercut. Purchases fell to 69,190 in 2023 and 57,020 in 2024, before edging up only slightly to 57,660 in 2025.1 That is 44% below the 2021 peak, and below the roughly 70,000-a-year average Hamptons records for 2015–19 — meaning the outflow has not just cooled from its pandemic high, it has dropped under the pre-pandemic norm. Independent research from the think tank Centre for Cities, using Office for National Statistics internal migration data, reached a similar conclusion in 2024: London's excess pandemic-era outflow was real but modest, "broadly an amplification of existing trends" rather than a new pattern, and showed clear signs of reversing as offices reopened.4

Key finding. The number of Londoners buying outside the capital in 2025 is not merely down from the pandemic spike — it is now below the level that prevailed, year after year, before the pandemic happened at all.
Why it happened, and why it stopped

Two forces are pulling movers back: converging prices, and the returning office

The first force is arithmetic. Over the five years to 2025, house prices outside London rose by 26%, more than triple the 8% recorded in the capital as a whole — and in Inner London specifically, prices barely moved, up just 2%.1 Independent, non-interested regional data points the same way: Nationwide's house price index recorded London as the weakest-performing region in Great Britain through 2024 and 2025, and the government's official UK House Price Index recorded an outright annual price fall of 1.2% in London in the year to November 2025, against continued growth nationally.3 A shrinking price gap means a smaller windfall for anyone selling in London to buy elsewhere — Hamptons calculates that the "space dividend" from leaving the capital, which could roughly triple a household's floor area in 2016, is now around a third smaller.1

The second force is the office. Hybrid working has not gone away: 28% of British workers hybrid-worked in the first quarter of 2025, according to the Office for National Statistics, up from around one in ten in the same quarter of 2021.2 But the terms of hybrid work have tightened. Job-postings data from the recruitment platform Indeed shows the share of UK hybrid roles requiring at least two office days a week rose from 65% in 2022 to 85% by 2025, squeezing out the one-day-a-week arrangements that once made a long commute tolerable.5 Hamptons attributes part of the reversal directly to this: "the return to office-based working has certainly played a role in keeping more Londoners rooted."1

The geography of who still leaves has shifted too. Inner Londoners now make up a record 30% of leavers, up from 25% a decade ago — while the destinations gaining the most ground are unglamorous commuter towns such as Thurrock, Hertsmere and Basildon, not the Cornwall or Cotswolds postcodes that dominated pandemic-era headlines.1 Hamptons reads this as a shift "from lifestyle to logic": those still moving look increasingly pushed by affordability rather than pulled by a rural fantasy.1

Where it's contested

Has the countryside dream ended for good, or just paused?

The case for a durable reversal

Centre for Cities' analysis of ONS migration data found no evidence of a permanent shift even during the pandemic itself, concluding the appeal of leaving "diminished as [London] reopened… and professionals were required to spend at least part of the week in the office."4 With office-attendance requirements still rising and the London–elsewhere price gap structurally wide, both drivers point the same way.1,5

Empirical dispute
The case for a rebound

Hamptons' own researchers caution that outmigration ticked up slightly in 2025 as transaction volumes across Britain rose 15% year-on-year, and note that "as the London market begins to gain momentum, we may see a modest rebound in outmigration."1 A cooling of London's five-year price underperformance could narrow the equity gap that is currently keeping people rooted.

Empirical dispute

Verdict. The reversal to date is a measured fact, not a projection: five straight years of falling or flat outmigration, now below the pre-pandemic norm. Whether that holds is a genuinely open empirical question the data available today cannot settle.

What we don't know

Hamptons' figures track home purchases only, via Land Registry and Connells Group transaction data — they say nothing about renters, who are a larger and faster-moving population and behaved differently in past cycles (one 2022 study found renter departures from London hit a decade high even as owner-occupier departures fell).6 Nor do they capture people who work remotely without ever formally relocating. No survey asks a representative sample of 2025 "London leavers" why they moved or stayed, so the balance between price arithmetic and office-attendance rules, as opposed to other factors, is the desk's reading of the available evidence rather than a directly measured cause. ONS hybrid-working figures are also not perfectly comparable month to month, as survey field periods have varied in length.2

  • Local absorption. This piece doesn't examine how individual destination towns such as Thurrock or Hertsmere are absorbing the buyers who do arrive, including any effect on local prices or school places.

Method. "Homes bought outside London by Londoners" is Hamptons' estate-agency estimate, built from Connells Group transaction records and Land Registry data, covering owner-occupier and investor/second-home purchases in England & Wales by London-resident buyers; it excludes rentals and does not capture remote movers who don't buy. Figures for prior years are periodically revised by Hamptons as later transaction data is reconciled, which is why some early press reports of a given year's total differ slightly from the figure now published for that year — this piece uses Hamptons' most recently published figure for each year. The five-year house-price growth comparison (outside London 26%, London 8%, Inner London 2%) is Hamptons' own index; its direction — London under-performing the rest of Great Britain — is independently corroborated by Nationwide's regional house price index and the government's UK House Price Index, both cited below, though neither publishes an identical five-year cumulative figure for direct like-for-like verification. ONS hybrid-working figures come from the Opinions and Lifestyle Survey; "hybrid" means working from home part of the time and travelling to work part of the time in the reference period. All percentage changes in this piece were computed independently in Node.js from the published figures and rechecked before publication. No projection or scenario appears in this piece; every figure shown is a measured, published estimate.
