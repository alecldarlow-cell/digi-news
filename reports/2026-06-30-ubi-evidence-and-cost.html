Economics · social policy

Basic income barely moved how much people work. The cost is the sticking point.

A decade of experiments — Helsinki, Texas, Stockton, rural Kenya — finds that unconditional cash reliably improves financial security and wellbeing, with small and mixed effects on employment. What none of them can settle is whether a country could afford to pay it to everyone at a level that would matter.

What the evidence shows

The idea has now been tested, repeatedly

A “universal basic income” (UBI) is a regular cash payment made to people unconditionally — no means test, no work requirement. Since 2016 a wave of trials has put versions of it to the test, following a smaller burst of income experiments in 1970s North America and, uniquely, the dividend that Alaska has paid to every resident since 1982.5 The designs vary enormously, from $22.50 a month in rural Kenya4 to £1,600 a month for young people leaving care in Wales.7

Two findings recur across almost all of them. Cash improves recipients’ financial security and mental wellbeing: in Finland, 55% of recipients rated their health good or very good against 46% of a control group,1 and US recipients spent the extra money mostly on rent, food and transport.2 And the effect on how much people work is small, and runs in both directions.

Key finding. Across the rigorous studies, no trial has produced the mass withdrawal from paid work that critics predict. The consistent, well-measured gains are in financial security and wellbeing — not in employment, which barely moves.
The work question

Does it stop people working?

The most rigorous test is OpenResearch’s study, which gave 1,000 low-income Americans $1,000 a month for three years and compared them with 2,000 controls. Recipients worked about 1.3 fewer hours a week and were roughly 2 percentage points less likely to be employed; their own income, excluding the transfer, fell by around $1,500 a year (a revised working paper puts the participation effect higher still).2 That is a real reduction — but a modest one, and the opposite has also been found. Finland’s recipients worked slightly more, about six extra days over the measured year.1 In Stockton, recipients moved into full-time work faster than controls (below). And in Alaska — the only universal, permanent scheme — four decades of data show no effect on total employment at all, only a shift toward part-time work.5 In rural Kenya, recipients did not stop working; many started businesses.4

One reading, offered by the researchers themselves, is that the time freed up went into caregiving, study and job-search rather than idleness, and recipients reported valuing work no less.2 But for the funding question that follows, the direction matters: where recipients’ own earnings fall, so does the tax revenue that would help pay for the scheme.

The cost

How would a country pay for it?

This is where basic income runs into arithmetic. Paid to everyone, even a modest sum is enormous in aggregate, so the honest question is the net cost after recycling the money through the tax system and replacing existing benefits. The OECD modelled exactly that: a budget-neutral basic income for the UK — funded by abolishing the personal tax-free allowance and most working-age benefits — would be worth about £230 a month, only a third of the poverty line.6 To pay a basic income at the level of today’s minimum-income safety net instead would need income-tax rates roughly a quarter higher.6

That is the core trade-off, and it is not about laziness: a basic income small enough to fund without large tax rises is too small to live on, while one big enough to matter requires very substantial tax rises and, because it is untargeted, cuts poverty less per pound than support aimed at those who need it.6 One escape route is to fund it from wealth rather than wages: Alaska pays its dividend from an oil-funded sovereign wealth fund, avoiding the work-disincentive worry — but the payment is only a few percent of income, nowhere near a living.5

What we don't know

No trial has tested a genuinely universal, permanent basic income set at a livable level in a rich economy — the version most debates are actually about. Alaska is universal and permanent but tiny; the generous schemes (Finland, OpenResearch, Wales) are temporary and targeted at specific groups, which limits what they reveal about a nationwide roll-out. Macro-economic effects — on inflation, wages and growth — are largely untested at national scale. And the impact findings from Britain’s own pilot in Wales are not due until 2027.7

Where it's contested

The case, and the counter-case

Basic income is genuinely disputed among credible experts. The disagreement has two strands: an empirical one about how people respond, where evidence is accumulating, and a value one about whether support should be universal or targeted, which evidence cannot settle.

The case for

Unconditional cash dependably raises financial security and wellbeing while barely denting work, and at population scale — Alaska — shows no aggregate employment loss.5 Universality means near-total take-up (97% in Wales),7 no stigma and no benefit cliff-edges, and a floor that holds up if automation erodes jobs.4

The counter-case

At any meaningful level it is hugely expensive and, being untargeted, a poor way to cut poverty: the same money concentrated on those in need helps them more.6 Recipients’ own earnings fall, eroding the tax base,2 and a self-funding version sits far below the poverty line.6

Empirical + value dispute
Design choices

What would make it worth considering

The evidence points less to a yes/no than to a set of levers that decide whether a basic income helps or merely costs. Level: below the poverty line it eases hardship but doesn’t end it; at the line it needs large tax rises. Replace or top up: rolling existing disability and housing support into a flat payment can leave the worst-off worse off, so a basic income added on top is safer but dearer.6 Funding: a sovereign-wealth dividend avoids work disincentives but is modest;5 income-tax funding is large but redistributive. Universal or targeted: universality buys full take-up and dignity; targeting buys more poverty reduction per pound.6

So a scheme would be worth serious consideration where the goal is a stable income floor robust to job disruption, where it tops up rather than replaces support for the most vulnerable, and where a durable, non-wage revenue source can fund it — conditions that make the popular image of a livable, universal, tax-funded payment the most expensive option, not the obvious one.

And the UK?

Britain could consider a basic income, but the binding constraint is fiscal, not behavioural. Its only live test is Wales’s care-leaver pilot — narrow, unusually generous, with impact results pending to 2027.7 Scotland’s 2018–2020 feasibility study concluded a pilot was desirable but not feasible under current devolved powers, with the key tax and benefit levers sitting at Westminster.8 The evidence needed to choose a level and a funding route for a country of nearly 70 million people9 simply does not yet exist — which is an argument for a serious UK trial before it is an argument for or against the policy.
