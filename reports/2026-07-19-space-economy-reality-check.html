Orbit got 96% cheaper. The industries built on that are mostly still on paper

Launching a kilogram to orbit costs 96% less than it did in 1960, and economists expect the fall to continue. That collapse is real and measured. But satellite refuelling, asteroid mining and orbital data centres — the industries cheap launch was meant to unlock — are still mostly demonstrations, failures and announcements, not revenue.

The number underneath everything else

The one chart that explains why this topic exists now

Every idea in this piece — mining an asteroid, refuelling a satellite, building a data centre in orbit — depends on the same underlying trend: it has become dramatically cheaper to get a kilogram of hardware into space. A July 2026 study led by Cambridge’s Bennett School of Public Policy and Turin’s Politecnico Institute, drawing on 4,405 launches between 1960 and 2025, put the average cost of reaching low Earth orbit at $87,023 per kilogram in 1960.1 That fell to roughly $20,000/kg by the early 1970s as Apollo-era production scaled up, then stalled for two decades once the Saturn V was retired and the Space Shuttle became the main US vehicle.1 It only began falling again after 1989, as Cold War-era state programmes gave way to commercial launch providers — and by 2025 the global average had reached $3,868/kg, a 96% fall since 1960.1

The same researchers project the fall will continue: to $1,569/kg by 2030 and $273/kg by 2040, if reusable heavy-lift vehicles such as SpaceX’s Starship reach routine, high-cadence flight.1 Those two later figures are a model’s central projection, not a measurement — they assume the cost curve continues its recent trajectory, which the researchers themselves flag is not guaranteed (see the caveat below).

Key finding. The cost of reaching orbit has already fallen 96% since 1960 — that part is measured history, not a forecast. Everything else in this piece is about how much of an industry has actually been built on top of it so far.
Who is actually driving the fall

The cost curve currently runs through one company

The 96% fall is a global average, but it is not evenly produced. SpaceX accounted for roughly 80% of all payload mass launched into orbit worldwide in 2025, on the same Cambridge/Politecnico dataset1 — a concentration the study’s authors compare, in a related working paper, to the East India Company’s 19th-century hold on maritime trade.2 That matters for everything that follows: nearly every business case in this piece assumes launch keeps getting cheaper on schedule, and that schedule currently depends heavily on one firm’s reusability programme continuing to work and continuing to sell capacity at close to today’s prices.

Where the hype gets ahead of the physics

Orbital data centres run into a cooling problem, not a power problem

Of the three industries this piece covers, orbital data centres have generated the most headlines relative to what has actually flown. Nvidia-backed Starcloud launched a 60kg satellite, Starcloud-1, in November 2025 carrying a single Nvidia H100 GPU — the first time a high-end AI chip has run in orbit — and used it to run inference and train a small language model.3 That is a genuine first, and a real measured fact. It is also a single GPU on a refrigerator-sized satellite, a long way from the 5-gigawatt facility the company has since pitched.4

The constraint is not power — sunlight in orbit is abundant and, outside eclipse, continuous. It is waste heat. In a vacuum there is no air or water to carry heat away by convection, so every watt a chip draws must eventually leave as infrared radiation, governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law.5 Engineering estimates published this year put the radiator area needed to reject a single megawatt of waste heat at roughly 1,200 to 1,600 square metres — several tennis courts to a hockey rink, per megawatt56 — and that radiator cannot simply be the back of the solar array, because a panel wants to face the sun while a radiator wants a cold, shaded view of deep space.6 A modern terrestrial AI training cluster draws power in the hundreds of megawatts; scaling that to orbit on current radiator physics implies radiator arrays measured in square kilometres, not square metres.

The signature exhibit

What has actually flown, versus what has only been announced

Strip away the market-size forecasts and the mission announcements, and the measured record across the four industries most often bundled into "the space economy" looks like this. Satellite servicing is the one genuine, repeated commercial success: Northrop Grumman’s MEV-1 docked with a commercial satellite in geostationary orbit in February 2020 — the first such commercial docking — extended its life for five years, then undocked in April 2025 to serve another customer; MEV-2 has been docked and providing service since April 2021 and was extended in 2024.7 In-space manufacturing has one clear success (Varda’s first capsule grew crystals of the drug ritonavir in orbit and returned them intact in February 2024) followed by five further missions that have mostly been repurposed toward hypersonic defence-sensor testing rather than pharmaceutical production.8 Refuelling has real hardware built and contracted, but as of this piece’s publication date it has not yet flown: Astroscale U.S.’s APS-R spacecraft is manifested to launch in summer 2026 to refuel two Space Force satellites via an Orbit Fab depot.9 Asteroid mining has the weakest record of the four: AstroForge’s first mission, Brokkr-1, reached orbit in 2023 but its refinery payload never activated; its second, Odin, launched in February 2025 and lost contact in deep space before reaching its target asteroid; a third mission, Vestri, is scheduled for late 2026 or 2027 to attempt the first-ever private landing on a body beyond the Earth-Moon system — landing only, not extraction.10

Key finding. Of the four verticals, only satellite servicing has a multi-year record of repeated, successful commercial missions. The other three have between zero and one qualified success each, against years of announcements.
What we don't know

The Terzi and Nicoli cost projections apply Wright’s Law — the empirical pattern that costs fall a fixed percentage with each doubling of cumulative output — forward from 2025; the paper itself flags that monopoly pricing power or geopolitical fragmentation could break that pattern, and no one can yet say which path is more likely.1 The two radiator estimates in Exhibit 03 come from published engineering analysis, not a built and measured facility at that scale; nothing close to a 1MW orbital data centre exists yet to check them against. No independent, audited figure exists for what it would actually cost to extract and process ore from an asteroid, because it has never been done — industry estimates of asteroid resource value (sometimes quoted in the trillions) describe the metal in place, not a recoverable, sellable quantity net of mission cost, and this piece has deliberately left such figures out rather than repeat them uncited. We also do not know whether Astroscale’s APS-R refuelling demonstration, manifested for summer 2026, will succeed by the time this piece is read; nor whether Varda’s pivot from pharmaceutical crystallisation toward defence and hypersonic-sensor payloads across its more recent missions reflects a permanent change in its business model or a temporary shift toward the more reliably funded customer.8

Where it's contested

Three genuine disagreements, not one manufactured one

Credible, informed sources disagree on several distinct questions here. They are not all the same kind of question, so we have labelled each.

The case

The cost curve keeps falling. Reusability is spreading beyond SpaceX — Blue Origin’s New Glenn and China’s emerging reusable boosters both target the same physics — and the study’s authors find the post-1989 rate of decline (44% per doubling of cumulative payload) is accelerating, not slowing, as commercial competition has replaced state monopolies.1

Empirical dispute
The counter-case

One firm still launches roughly 80% of world tonnage. The same researchers warn that a profit-maximising near-monopoly, combined with rising geopolitical fragmentation as nations build parallel, protected launch capacity, could stall or reverse the trend well before 2040 — the projection is a central case, not a guarantee.12

Empirical dispute
The case

The falling-cost logic already supports real, if narrow, businesses: satellite life-extension has a five-year commercial track record,7 and in-space pharmaceutical manufacturing has produced at least one verified result.8 On this reading, mining and orbital data centres are simply earlier on the same curve servicing already climbed.

Empirical dispute
The counter-case

Servicing works because it solves a narrow, well-defined problem (keep an existing satellite flying) using mature docking technology. Mining and data centres face open engineering problems — unproven in-space ore processing, and cooling physics that scale badly with size — that cheaper launch alone does not solve; three years of asteroid-mining attempts have yet to return a qualified success.106

Empirical dispute
The US/Artemis Accords position

The 2015 US Space Resource Exploration and Utilization Act entitles American citizens to resources they extract in space, and the 69-nation Artemis Accords state that extracting space resources does not, by itself, amount to the "national appropriation" the 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits — extraction is framed as permitted "use," not forbidden ownership of the celestial body itself.11

Legal dispute — resolvable in principle, but untested
The treaty-literalist position

Russia and China, both non-signatories, have called the Accords a unilateral US attempt to write international space law to its own advantage; legal scholars note the Outer Space Treaty is binding as customary international law on all states regardless of signature, and that whether extraction on this scale counts as "appropriation" has never been tested by any court or tribunal.1211

Legal dispute — resolvable in principle, but untested

What this piece left out

  • Lunar ISRU and Artemis. NASA’s Artemis programme and its plans to extract water ice near the lunar south pole are a distinct, government-led effort from the private mining and manufacturing ventures covered here, with its own funding, timeline and technical record. It deserves its own piece.
  • China’s International Lunar Research Station. China and Russia are building a parallel lunar and resource framework outside the Artemis Accords; we noted the legal disagreement but did not assess ILRS’s own technical progress.
  • Orbital debris. More launches and more in-space servicing traffic raise collision and debris risk; we did not quantify it here.
  • Space solar power. Beaming solar energy from orbit to Earth is a further idea the falling-cost logic could unlock; it remains earlier-stage than any of the four verticals covered here and was left out for length.
  • Full market-size forecasts. We deliberately did not repeat commercial market-research projections for asteroid mining or orbital manufacturing revenue; several circulating figures could not be traced to a transparent, checkable methodology and were excluded rather than cited uncritically.
Method. Launch-cost figures (1960, early-1970s plateau, 2025, and the 2030/2040 projections) are taken directly from Terzi & Nicoli, PNAS Nexus (July 2026), a standardised dataset of 4,405 launches from 1960–2025 across sixteen spacefaring regions; the 2030 and 2040 figures are the paper’s own central model projection under Wright’s Law and are rendered dashed in Exhibit 01, never used as a hero stat or headline figure. Percentage falls (95.6% for 1960→2025, 59.4% for 2025→2030, 92.9% for 2025→2040) were computed in Node.js from the paper’s printed figures and checked against the paper’s own rounded statements (“~96%”, “more than 58%”, “~93%”). Exhibit 04’s hatched spans mark contracted, publicly announced missions not yet flown as of 19 July 2026 — a factual publication-date cutoff, not a modelled scenario; every other span is a completed, reported mission event. Radiator-area figures in Exhibit 03 are independent published engineering estimates for radiating 1MW of waste heat at roughly 20°C via the Stefan-Boltzmann law; no orbital facility at that scale has been built, so both are estimates, not measurements, and are presented as a range rather than a single number for that reason. This piece contains no projection, scenario or modelled figure in its headline, standfirst, or hero stats; the only projected figures anywhere are the 2030/2040 launch-cost points, which are explicitly labelled and visually dashed throughout.
