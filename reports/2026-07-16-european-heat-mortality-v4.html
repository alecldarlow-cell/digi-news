The heat was measured to two decimal places. The death toll is two different numbers.

Western Europe averaged 20.74°C in June — a record, and 3.06°C above normal. For the single week the heat peaked, Europe has two figures for the dead: 10,650 and 20,390. One starts from registered deaths. The other never looks at one.

What was measured

The thermometer is exact. The sentence built from it is a choice.

June 2026 was western Europe’s warmest June on record, the Copernicus Climate Change Service reports: an average of 20.74°C, 3.06°C above the 1991–2020 average for the month, beating June 2025.1 That is a measurement, reported to a hundredth of a degree.

It is also a sentence with two dials on it. The first is the box. “Western Europe” here means 11°W–15°E, 37°–55°N — roughly Spain and Britain east to Italy and Germany.1 Widen to all European land (25°W–40°E, 34°N–72°N) and the same month, in the same dataset, comes in at 19.14°C, 1.78°C above average — and second-warmest, behind June 2019.1 The box alone moves the anomaly by 1.28°C, and the record from first to second.

The second dial is the calendar. May was violent: an early heatwave from about 21 to 30 May broke May records in France, the UK, Ireland and Portugal.3 Yet May 2026 ranks only seventh-warmest for Europe, at 13.87°C — because, as Copernicus states plainly, a colder-than-average first half balanced the monthly mean.3 Move the month boundary and the same air produces a record or an also-ran.

Key finding. Every figure above is measured, sourced and correct. They support four different headlines about the same summer. The record is real — and it is also a statement about a rectangle and a calendar month.
What was not measured

Two numbers for one week

Between 22 and 28 June the heat peaked. Europe now has two figures for what it cost, and they are not two attempts at the same sum.

The first is 10,650: EuroMOMO’s pooled estimate of excess all-cause deaths that week across 27 European countries or subnational regions. The network is hosted by Denmark’s Statens Serum Institut and backed by the ECDC and the WHO.4 It starts from deaths that were registered, and subtracts the number expected. More than 9,000 were aged 65 or over — and over the preceding eight weeks the same countries had been running about 500 deaths a week below expected.9

The second is 20,390: a preprint by Christopher Callahan of Indiana University Bloomington, posted on 30 June, which applies previously developed heat–mortality response functions to the same week’s temperatures.5 It is not a count and does not claim to be. It infers deaths from how hot it was, using a relationship fitted to earlier events, and it has not been peer-reviewed.

So the ratio — 1.91 times, a gap of 9,740 — is not a scandal. It is what happens when one instrument counts bodies and subtracts an expectation, and another reads a thermometer and consults a curve. Neither is the toll.

Down one level

Even the national figure is not what it looks like

France is the cleanest test: the country June hit hardest, and the one with the most-cited number. Santé publique France recorded 8,973 deaths from all causes in the week of 22–28 June, against 6,948 the week before — 2,025 more, a rise of 29.1%.6 That has been widely reported as excess deaths. It is not. It is a comparison with the previous week, not with an expected baseline; France’s actual excess-mortality estimate appears three weeks after a heatwave ends, once about 95% of deaths are consolidated.6

It is also built from electronic death certificates, which the agency’s own epidemiologist notes cover roughly 60% of deaths nationally — and unevenly: about 25% of deaths at home, 45% in nursing homes, close to 80% in hospitals.10 The most striking line in the bulletin is a 91% rise in deaths at home.6 That is the stratum the system sees least well.

Callahan’s model puts France at 5,210 for the same seven days.5 Same country, same week, 2.6 times apart — again two measures, not one disagreement. The desk’s reading, offered as analysis: no published figure today is a count of people killed by heat in France, and none claims to be.

What we don’t know

No death certificate says “heatwave”. Excess mortality is a residual against a modelled expectation, not a cause of death — so even the registered figures here are partly modelled, at the baseline. What this piece could not establish: (a) the Callahan preprint’s 95% confidence interval — the PDF could not be machine-retrieved and the interval circulating online traces only to a tertiary aggregator, so it is omitted rather than printed; the country split comes from consistent reporting of the preprint by three outlets.11 (b) A second estimate of about 15,000, attributed to a researcher at Poznań University of Medical Sciences, could not be traced to any primary or press source, and is not used. (c) EuroMOMO publishes no country breakdown and flags that recent weeks may move. (d) The UK Health Security Agency’s registered estimate for England and Wales is still to come, and will test the modelled 2,700.8 (e) Whether Europe’s heat-health warnings cut this event’s toll below what a function fitted on past heatwaves predicts — possibly part of the gap; nobody has shown it either way.

Where it’s contested

How much life did the heat actually take?

One question here is genuinely open among credible researchers, and it decides what any of these numbers mean: whether heat deaths are lives cut short by years, or by days. The effect is called mortality displacement, or harvesting. Peer-reviewed estimates of its size differ across most of the available range.12

Displacement is large

Heat kills disproportionately among the very frail, and a spike is often followed by a dip as deaths are pulled forward by days or weeks. A study of 15 European cities put harvesting at around 70% of heat-associated deaths; a European analysis found heat impacts cut by about 75% once displacement was allowed for. On this reading a weekly excess figure badly overstates life-years lost — and EuroMOMO’s eight preceding weeks running ~500 below expected is the pattern that invites the question.129

Empirical dispute
Displacement is small

Analyses of the 2003 French heatwave put displacement under 10% of deaths. Daily time-series methods can only detect harvesting at short lags; studies using annual windows find temperature effects operating at much longer lags than daily studies can see, which suggests the deaths are not merely rescheduled. On this reading the excess is close to real loss.12

Empirical dispute

Evidence can narrow this: it turns on mortality in the months after June 2026 — measurable, not yet measured. Digi News states no bottom line. Note the scale: the range in the literature, roughly 10% to 70%, is wider than the gap between the two headline death figures above.

Caveat — the strongest objection to this piece. Setting 10,650 against 20,390 risks implying a controversy that does not exist. They are different measures over different areas; a gap is expected. A reader could leave thinking science is confused about heat deaths when it is not. The defence: both were reported as June’s death toll without that distinction — and the distinction is the story. Second objection: the piece leans on EuroMOMO’s week-26 estimate, which the network says may be revised.4

Method. Temperature figures are ERA5 monthly means and anomalies against 1991–2020, taken from the C3S bulletins for May and June 2026; area definitions are printed as C3S states them. Two wrinkles are worth naming. C3S’s own June press release gives the western Europe anomaly as 3.06°C, while the WMO and some syndications render it 3.05°C; both are roundings of the same value and we print the issuing body’s figure. C3S also uses two southern bounds for “western Europe” — 37°N for the June monthly anomaly chart, 34°N for the May daily time series — so we quote the bound attached to the number we print. Derived figures were computed in Node and matched to source before printing: 8,973 − 6,948 = 2,025 and +29.1% (Santé publique France); the modelled-to-registered ratio 20,390 ÷ 10,650 = 1.91 and the 9,740 gap; the 1.28°C box effect and 1.18°C calendar effect in Exhibit 01. No figure in this piece was estimated by eye. Per §3, the Callahan estimate is a model output: it is held out of the headline, the standfirst and all three hero stats, is stored as a separate series, and renders hatched in Exhibit 02. The 10,650 hero figure is registered excess against a modelled baseline, which is stated at the point of use. Where sources measure different things we have said so rather than reconciling them.
