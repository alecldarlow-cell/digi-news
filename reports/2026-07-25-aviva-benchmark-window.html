Aviva beat the market by 11 points. It did not beat its own sector.

Between its 5 June low and 24 July the insurer returned 14.6% against a FTSE 100 up 3.6%. Over the five weeks in which its sector index can be measured alongside it, Aviva lagged that index. The gap is a benchmark artefact, not company news.

Executive summary

A large gap against the market, and a benchmark that explains it

Aviva's ordinary shares closed at 691.00p on Friday 24 July, up 87.8p — 14.56% — from 603.20p on 5 June, the lowest close of that month.321 Over the same 35 sessions the FTSE 100 rose 3.55%.5 The gap is roughly 11 percentage points. On Aviva's share count that is about £1.96bn of market value, and it is large enough to demand an explanation.

Almost all of that gap is accounted for by the choice of comparator, and none of it by anything Aviva did. The FTSE 100 is not what Aviva competes with. Measured against the FTSE 350 Life Insurance index — the sector classification Aviva sits in — over the five weeks in which this desk could obtain both series, Aviva returned 6.93% against the index's 7.08%.4 It lagged.

Swapping one benchmark for another is doing heavy work in that sentence, so this piece interrogates the substitute rather than resting on it. The result survives moving the start date. The index itself is a looser fit than its name suggests.

Key finding. Against the FTSE 100 from 5 June, Aviva's excess return is +11.01 percentage points. Against its own sector index over the longest window in which both series are observable, it is −0.15 percentage points. Changing the comparator, not the company, removes the anomaly.
Establishing the delta

Eleven points against the market, and what they are worth

The arithmetic is straightforward. Applying the FTSE 100's 3.55% return to Aviva's 5 June close of 603.20p gives an index-matched price of 624.62p on 24 July. Aviva printed 691.00p. The difference is 66.38p a share.3

Aviva's market capitalisation was reported at £20.45bn at the 24 July close, implying roughly 2.96bn shares in issue.6 On that basis the seven-week rise added about £2.60bn of market value in total, of which some £1.96bn is in excess of what the broad index delivered. That is the quantity the rest of this piece tries to account for.

Carrying the index forward at a beta of 1.0 is a deliberate simplification: no beta was estimated. A beta above 1.0 would shrink the residual, below 1.0 would widen it. Read it as an index-matched comparison, not a risk-adjusted one.

Explaining the delta · 1

Against its own sector index, the gap is gone

UK life insurers re-rated hard through July. The FTSE 350 Life Insurance index rose 7.08% between 25 June and 24 July and set a 52-week high of 8,554.46 intraday on 22 July.4 Over a near-identical month the FTSE 100 rose 2.98%.5 The sector ran at more than twice the market.

Aviva went with it, and slightly behind it. Rebasing both series to 100 at the 25 June close, Aviva ends the window at 106.93 and the index at 107.08. The two lines are close enough throughout that no eye would separate them without the numbers.

This is the single most consequential substitution in the piece. An 11-point divergence from the FTSE 100 becomes a 0.15-point shortfall against the life insurance sector. The move was real; it was not Aviva's.

It is worth being precise about what that benchmark is, because the name promises more than the construction delivers. The index has seven constituents: Aviva, Legal & General, Phoenix, Prudential, Aberdeen, St. James's Place and Old Mutual.22 Three of those are UK bulk annuity writers. Prudential is a predominantly Asian life insurer, Aberdeen is an asset manager, St. James's Place is a wealth manager, and Old Mutual is listed in Johannesburg as well as London.

So the comparison is directionally right and technically loose at the same time. It is the classification Aviva sits in, and it is much closer to Aviva's business than the FTSE 100 is. It is not a clean portfolio of annuity competitors, and the rates-and-annuities explanation set out below applies cleanly to three of its seven members. Readers should treat the −0.15 point figure as evidence that Aviva moved with a broadly similar group, not as a precise measurement against its competitive set.

Robustness

The finding holds from every start date available

A single window can flatter a result, so the sector comparison was re-run from every date on which both series can be observed. It survives. Aviva is behind the index from 25 June, 26 June and 2 July, and ahead by five hundredths of a percentage point from 10 July.34 Its largest shortfall, 1.09 points from 2 July, is more than twenty times its largest lead.

The 5 June anchor is this desk's choice, and worth stating plainly rather than defending: it is the lowest close of that month, 2.13% above the 52-week low of 590.60p.6 It was not a one-session dip — Aviva closed between 612p and 631p through May and slid steadily into it — but any window anchored on an observed minimum will flatter the measured excess return. That is a reason to check the result against other start dates, which is what the exhibit below does, rather than a second explanation standing alongside the benchmark.

Set this against the market comparison. From 5 June, measured against the FTSE 100, Aviva's excess return is +11.01 percentage points.5 Measured against its sector index, on every start date available, it sits between −1.09 and +0.05. The anomaly lives in the choice of comparator, not in the company.
The limit of the evidence

Half the rise happened before the benchmark series begins

There is a real hole in this analysis and it should be stated plainly rather than papered over. The FTSE 350 Life Insurance daily series held by this desk begins on 25 June.4 Aviva rose 7.13% between 5 June and 25 June and 6.93% between 25 June and 24 July: 49% of the 87.8p gain falls in the leg that has not been compared with the sector.

That is also, by construction, the leg immediately following a near-52-week low, and therefore the leg where a recovery bounce would be expected to be largest. Both a sector explanation and a company-specific one are consistent with the data in hand for that period. This desk cannot separate them and does not claim to.

Why the sector moved

Rates, a change of government, and a structural demand story

Three things were happening to UK life insurers over these seven weeks. They operate on different timescales, and only the first is tied to the window itself.

1. Gilt yields, and a change of prime minister

Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July and said he would seek flexibility within the existing fiscal rules. Gilts sold off the same day, the 30-year yield rising 9 basis points to 5.75%, a two-month high.9 The 10-year yield passed 5% late on 20 July and stayed above it all week — the longest such stretch since July 2008 — with oil near $100 a barrel compounding the pressure.10 The sector index set its 52-week high two days later.4

Higher long-dated yields are conventionally read as helpful to annuity writers: better reinvestment yields, and better scheme funding levels, which brings more buy-in business to market. This desk reports that reading rather than endorsing it. No source consulted here isolates the transmission from gilt yields to UK life insurance equity prices over this specific window, and the relationship has not been stable in recent years. The timing is suggestive; it is not a demonstrated cause.

2. The bulk annuity market

Underneath the rates move sits the sector's main earnings engine. Moody's has estimated that annuities accounted for 71% of the combined operating earnings of the six largest UK bulk annuity providers between 2022 and 2024.19 The Prudential Regulation Authority describes the life sector as showing rapid growth in bulk purchase annuities, and has made that market — alongside insurers' growing use of funded reinsurance — a supervisory priority for 2026.14

How much business that market will actually produce this year is disputed, and the dispute matters for whether a re-rating is justified. It is set out below.

3. Regulation, pulling the other way

On 29 April the PRA opened consultation CP8/26, proposing to recalibrate the capital treatment of funded reinsurance under Solvency UK, more closely aligning counterparty default risk with how insurers treat comparable investments.15 Law firm analysis of the proposal suggests it could double or triple the capital required for an average funded reinsurance transaction.16 Funded reinsurance is one of the tools that has let annuity writers grow balance sheets quickly, so this is a headwind, not a tailwind — and it was already public before the window began.

Two macro data points also landed on the window's final session. On 24 July the S&P Global flash UK composite PMI returned to growth at 52.1, from 49.3 in June,2 and June retail sales rose 1% against an expected fall.13 Both are supportive of domestically exposed financials, and both landed on the day the FTSE 100 rose 0.91% to 10,736.23.7

What none of this explains is anything specific to Aviva — which is consistent with the finding that there is nothing specific to Aviva in the measurable data.

Where it's contested

How big is the 2026 annuity market?

The sector re-rated on a view about future business volumes. Credible forecasters who work inside that market disagree about those volumes by a wide margin. Both figures below are forecasts, not measured data, and both come from firms with a commercial interest in the market they are forecasting.

Demand is exceptional

WTW forecasts total UK pension risk transferred to insurers and reinsurers reaching around £70bn in 2026, roughly 15% above 2025, with the bulk annuity market alone exceeding £50bn on larger deal sizes and attractive pricing.17 The PRA's own supervisory language describes rapid growth in bulk purchase annuities.14

Empirical dispute · forecast
Demand is narrower

Standard Life forecasts total bulk annuity volumes of only £35bn to £40bn for 2026, noting that the first half was dominated by a large number of smaller schemes: transaction counts up substantially year on year, but total deal volume slightly down.18 On that reading the market is busier but not bigger.18

Empirical dispute · forecast

This desk takes no view on which forecast is closer. The relevant point for this piece is narrower: a sector-wide argument about sector-wide volumes is not a reason for one constituent to diverge from the others, and in the measurable data Aviva did not.

Testing an alternative

Could undisclosed stake-building explain it? The turnover makes it unlikely

A common explanation for an unexplained rise is a buyer accumulating below the disclosure threshold. Under the FCA's Disclosure Guidance and Transparency Rules, a holder of shares in a UK issuer must notify at 3% of voting rights.1 On roughly 2.96bn shares, 3% is about 88.8m shares.

Aviva traded 239.9m shares across the 35 sessions of the window, an average of 6.85m a day.323 A build to just under that threshold would therefore be about 37% of everything that changed hands. A buyer taking a realistic slice of daily volume — 15% to 20% is a common assumption for accumulation intended to avoid moving the price — would need between 65 and 86 sessions. The window contains 35.

The test rests on an assumed participation rate that a conspicuous buyer could exceed, and it cannot exclude a single block crossed off-book, which would leave no signature in daily turnover. This desk also did not audit Aviva's regulatory news record and makes no claim about what has or has not been filed.

What can be said is narrower and firmer: the price data offers no support for the hypothesis, because there is no company-specific outperformance in the measurable window for accumulation to explain. Aviva's shortfall against its sector over those five weeks, 0.15 percentage points, is about 1.0p a share — smaller than the stock's 6.4p trading range on 24 July alone.6

What we don't know

The first three weeks. The sector index series held here starts on 25 June, and 49% of Aviva's gain came before it. Whether Aviva outperformed, matched or lagged its sector between 5 and 25 June is not established by anything in this piece.

Why it starts there, which is not a good reason. This is a retrieval limit, not a publication limit. The provider's historical page renders a rolling one-month window by default, and on a 25 July retrieval that window opens on 25 June.4 The date range is adjustable, and was adjusted for the Aviva series, which runs from 26 May.3 It was not adjusted for the index. The earlier data exists and is obtainable; this piece simply does not have it, and a re-pull would close the gap outright. That is the single most useful thing a reader could hold against this analysis.

One partial check, which is not a finding. Legal & General, the sector's second-largest constituent by market value, can be observed from 12 June. Over the ten sessions from 12 to 25 June, Aviva returned 2.93% and Legal & General 2.32% — Aviva ahead by 0.61 percentage points.20 That is one stock standing in for a seven-company index over part of the missing leg, which is precisely the kind of proxy this desk declined to build a conclusion on. It is recorded because it exists, not because it settles anything.

Risk adjustment. No beta, volatility or correlation estimate was computed, because the daily series required were not obtained in full. Every comparison here is a raw return difference. Readers should not treat the residuals as statistically significant or insignificant — this piece makes no significance claim in either direction.

Causation in the sector move. The gilt-yield explanation for the life sector's re-rating is a widely held reading, not a finding. No source consulted isolates cause over this window.

Provenance of the price data. Closing prices and index levels were taken from commercial market-data redistributors rather than direct from the exchange or index provider. Aviva's full daily series has been replicated against a second independent provider: 34 of the 35 closes agree exactly, and the exception is 20 July, printed at 672.00p by one and 671.80p by the other.323 The 0.20p difference does not move any figure in this piece. The sector index series has not been replicated, because no second provider carrying its daily history was found.

By the numbers

The move shows up in the income as well as the price. On a trailing 2025 dividend of 39.3p a share,12 Aviva's yield fell from 6.52% at the 5 June close to 5.69% at 691p — 83 basis points in seven weeks. One reading is that this is what a sector-wide fall in required return looks like from inside a single stock. It is not, on its own, evidence of anything Aviva did.

Four strands were deliberately left out to hold the piece to its evidence.

  • Reconstructing the June sector leg. The 5–25 June gap can be bridged using a single large constituent as a proxy. This desk judged a one-stock proxy for a seven-company index too weak to carry a headline finding and dropped it rather than caveat it.
  • Risk-adjusted attribution. A beta-and-residual decomposition would put a significance figure on each comparison. It requires full daily series for all three instruments, which were not obtained.
  • Constituent-level attribution. Comparing Aviva against each sector member individually, rather than against the index, would show whether the sector move was broad or concentrated.
  • Valuation. The implied change in cost of equity across the window, and the analyst consensus target of 693.46p against the 691.00p close, are noted but not developed here.6
