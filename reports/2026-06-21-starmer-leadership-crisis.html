Two years after a landslide, Keir Starmer’s leadership is in open crisis

The Observer reports the prime minister is expected to set out a timetable for his departure on Monday; a government source told Reuters he remains focused on the job, and Downing Street has not confirmed anything.5 Whatever Monday brings, the measured picture is already stark: near-record-low ratings, the heaviest local-election losses in a generation, and a rival newly installed in Parliament with a route to the top.

What the data shows

A steep, early and broad-based fall in support

Labour won the July 2024 general election with a Commons majority of 172 — but on the smallest share of the vote of any majority government in modern record-keeping.3 That thin foundation eroded quickly. Starmer’s net favourability began marginally positive and fell across his premiership; by January 2026 YouGov put it at roughly −57, a level it otherwise records only for Liz Truss, and it sat near −45 in mid-June 2026.1 The government’s own net approval was about −51 in June, leaving Labour almost as unpopular as the Conservatives were on the eve of their 2024 defeat.1

The discontent came from several directions at once. Early policy choices set the tone: a means-test on the winter fuel payment for pensioners — later largely reversed in 2025 — was the single least-popular early measure, and surveys soon showed majorities unhappy on immigration, the NHS and an early-release prisons policy.18 Critics on the right attacked tax rises and small-boats numbers; critics on the left objected to welfare reform, the government’s stance on the Gaza war and its refusal of a wealth tax.8 Pollster Luke Tryl of More in Common described Starmer as having “become a vessel for people’s frustration with the system”.8

A separate scandal compounded matters: the appointment, then September 2025 dismissal, of Peter Mandelson — a friend of the late convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein — as ambassador to Washington, and questions over what Starmer knew and when. Starmer never met Epstein and is not accused of wrongdoing.7

Why the pressure has peaked now

The verdict at the ballot box, then inside the party

Unpopularity became an existential threat when it showed up in votes. At the May 2026 local elections Labour lost control of around 35 councils and close to 1,500 councillors; the BBC’s projected national vote share put the party on about 17% — roughly half its general-election level, and level-pegging for third.6 By mid-May, more than 95 Labour MPs had publicly urged Starmer to resign or set a departure date, and a health secretary, four junior ministers and several aides had quit.8 The threat sharpened on 18 June, when Greater Manchester mayor Andy Burnham — long seen as the most plausible alternative — won the Makerfield by-election with 54.8% of the vote and a majority of about 9,200, returning to Parliament and making himself eligible to stand in any leadership contest.4

The public verdict is unambiguous in the most recent polling. In an Ipsos survey of 1,142 GB adults conducted 12–16 June 2026, 52% said Starmer should stand down as prime minister against 35% who said he should continue; two-thirds — 67% — said he should not lead Labour into the next general election, and just 8% thought the party more likely to win with him than without.2

In historical context

Six prime ministers in ten years

A leader losing public confidence and being pushed out by their own party between elections is, in itself, an ordinary feature of Britain’s parliamentary system: voters elect MPs, not a prime minister directly, and a governing party can replace its leader at any time.7 What is unusual is the pace. In the 26 years from 1979 to 2005 the UK had three prime ministers; from 2005 to 2015, three more; since 2016 it has had six — four of them in the four years to 2024.8 If Starmer departs, he becomes the sixth to leave office in a decade, and a successor would be the seventh prime minister in ten years.7

The contrast is sharpest when turnover is read as a rate. Three leaders across 26 years is one about every nine years; three across the next decade is one every three; six in the most recent decade is one roughly every 20 months.8

Starmer is, in one respect, an outlier even within this turbulent run. His four immediate predecessors were largely “takeover” prime ministers who inherited crises mid-Parliament; of them, only Rishi Sunak lost a general election, while Theresa May, Boris Johnson and Liz Truss were effectively removed by their own MPs.3 Starmer, by contrast, won a landslide just two years ago — and the last two leaders to win by comparable margins, Margaret Thatcher and Tony Blair, each governed for around a decade.3

What we don’t know

Whether — and when — Starmer actually resigns is unconfirmed: The Observer reports an expected Monday statement, while a government source says he remains focused on governing, and there is no Downing Street confirmation.5 Nor is it settled whether a formal contest will be triggered (a challenger needs 81 nominations) or who would win it. The causes of his unpopularity are many and overlapping — the economy, Brexit’s long shadow, specific policies and scandals — and polling cannot cleanly isolate which mattered most. Ratings here are snapshots; YouGov’s favourability figures come from online panels for which no classical margin of error applies, and small wave-to-wave differences should not be read as real movement.

Where it’s genuinely contested

Is rapid turnover a sign of a healthy democracy?

On the facts of how it happens, there is little dispute: mid-term removal of a leader is constitutional and routine in Westminster systems. On what it signifies, credible observers genuinely disagree — partly an empirical question about consequences, partly a value question about what we want from democracy.

Reads as accountability

That a party can remove a leader who has lost public confidence — without a country waiting years for the next election — is the system being responsive, not breaking down. Voters do not elect a PM directly; the mechanism that lets MPs act on a collapse in support is a feature, not a fault.7

Empirical & value
Reads as instability

Frequent changes weaken the office, break policy continuity and can signal a deeper malaise — weak party loyalty, a stagnant post-Brexit economy, leaders unable to deliver. Landslide winners failing within two years is historically abnormal and, on this view, a symptom of dysfunction rather than healthy renewal.37

Empirical & value
Caveat. Two cautions on any tidy reading. First, Starmer’s ratings, though very poor, were not quite as low as Johnson’s, Sunak’s or Truss’s immediately before they fell — so the numbers alone do not fully explain the pressure; party management and Burnham’s emergence matter too.9 Second, because he is a landslide winner rather than a mid-term takeover, his case may be partly idiosyncratic, which weakens any claim that his troubles prove a purely structural instability.3
