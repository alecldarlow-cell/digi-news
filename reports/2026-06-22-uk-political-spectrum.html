Britain leaned left and liberal. Its politics turned right anyway.

The left–right spectrum can be measured — Britain has done it the same way since 1986. On those yardsticks the public's average economic and social attitudes have drifted left and become markedly more liberal over the past 15 years. Yet a right-wing populist party has just posted its best-ever result and now leads the polls. What changed most is not the centre of gravity, but the shape of the divide.

Can it be quantified?

Forty years of the British spectrum, on one scale

The intuition that a country has “moved right” is testable. Since 1986 the British Social Attitudes survey has placed people on two value scales, each built from a fixed battery of agree/disagree statements and scored from 0 to 100: an economic left–right scale (redistribution, business, fairness) and a liberal–authoritarian scale (rules, order, personal freedom).15 Read together, they are the closest thing Britain has to a thermometer of where the public sits.

Two different stories emerge. Economic attitudes are cyclical: Britain edged right under New Labour — the score peaked near 42 around 2004 and was still 41 in 2010 — then swung back. By 2022 it stood at 33, close to where it was in the mid-1990s.1 Social attitudes show a one-way, secular shift: the liberal–authoritarian score held around 69–71 for a quarter-century, then fell to 56 by 2022 — a sustained move toward a more liberal outlook on sex, family and personal choice.1 The one recent wrinkle, and the kernel of truth in the “moved right” feeling, is a small reversal: the score edged back up to 59 by 2025, the first clear turn toward the authoritarian end in the series.1

Key finding. On the two long-run measures of British values, the public's average position in 2022 was to the left of, and more liberal than, its position around 2010 — the opposite of a rightward drift in attitudes.
Where it widened

The spectrum did stretch — by age, not by a shared shift right

There is a real sense in which the spectrum has widened. For decades, young and old held almost identical average economic views: in 1986 both the under-35s and the over-55s scored 37, and as recently as 2019 both scored 38.1 Then they parted. By 2022 the under-35s had moved sharply left to 28, while the over-55s barely moved, at 36 — opening an eight-point gap where there had been none.1 The widening is driven by the young moving left, not by the country moving right.

Your instinct that younger people have grown more liberal while older generations have not is broadly right — and it is the deeper engine of the whole picture. Throughout the survey's 40 years, the young have always been the more liberal; the country liberalises chiefly because each new generation enters more liberal than the one it replaces.1 Crucially, NatCen's age–cohort–period modelling finds this is a birth-cohort effect, not an ageing one: people do not generally turn conservative as they get older, they largely keep the outlook they formed young.1 Take one marker of liberal family values — disagreeing that people who want children should marry. Just 5% of those born in the 1920s took that view; 41% of the 1960s cohort do (almost unchanged from the 43% they recorded back in 1994); and 55% of those born in the 1990s.1

Consistent with the bigger picture. Generational replacement is exactly why a country can grow steadily more socially liberal even when few individuals change their minds — and why that drift is likely to continue as older cohorts shrink.
A second dimension

What really changed: values now predict the vote far more strongly

The bigger structural change is not where people sit but how their values map onto parties. British politics used to run mainly along the economic axis. Now a cultural axis — liberal versus authoritarian, the dimension that tracks Brexit, immigration and identity — is roughly as decisive.1 In 2015, libertarians were 21 points more likely than authoritarians to back Labour, the Liberal Democrats or the Greens; by 2024–25 that gap had doubled to 42 points. The mirror gap on the right — authoritarians favouring the Conservatives or Reform — widened from 19 to 32 points.1

Values or protest?

Are voters swayed off core issues — or onto a new one?

This is the heart of your question: with the same economic and social scales, can we see whether voters are still moved by bread-and-butter interests, or pulled away by identity and culture-war debates? NatCen's anatomy of the Reform vote is the cleanest test. Support for Reform is sharply sorted by the identity (liberal–authoritarian) scale — 34% of the most authoritarian third back it against just 5% of the most liberal, a 29-point gap — and by welfare attitudes (a 21-point gap). But it is barely sorted by the classic economic left–right scale at all: a 6-point gap, with only 37% of Reform supporters even sitting on the economic right.1 The party's surge since 2024 came overwhelmingly from the authoritarian end (up 12 points there, against 4 among liberals).1

So voters have not simply abandoned their material interests for populist theatre. Reform supporters are more discontented — more likely to be struggling financially and dissatisfied with the NHS — but NatCen's modelling finds that their ideology, and the identity axis above all, predicts support far more strongly than discontent does.1 The honest summary: a second, cultural axis now does much of the mobilising that economics used to, cross-cutting the old left–right line rather than erasing it.

The fault line that did widen

Graduates and non-graduates — the modern cleavage

If one division has genuinely opened, it is education. Support for Reform climbs steeply as qualifications fall — from 9% of graduates to 40% of those whose highest qualification is below A-level — while the Greens, Labour and the Liberal Democrats lean the opposite way.1 It is the modern analogue of the old class divide, but it now runs more on identity than on pay: graduates cluster at the liberal pole, non-graduates at the authoritarian one.

And the ground is shifting under it. The share of working-age adults classed as graduates (above A-level) has roughly doubled — from 24% in 2002 to 42% by 2017.9 Because graduates are the most socially liberal and least Reform-leaning group, this steady expansion keeps enlarging the liberal pole by sheer weight of numbers. That is a large part of why the population's social average drifts liberal across the decades even as the populist-right vote climbs — the right is winning a bigger share of a group (older non-graduates) that is itself shrinking as a proportion of the country.

So why does it feel more right-wing?

Because the electoral right grew, even as average values did not

The feeling is not imaginary — it is mostly about votes, not values. The principal right-wing populist vehicle (UKIP, then the Brexit Party, now Reform UK) took 12.6% in 2015, fell away in 2017 and 2019 as the Conservatives absorbed the Leave vote, then surged to 14.3% in 2024: the best general-election share a right-wing populist party has ever won in Britain.23 Researchers note the 2024 Reform vote was almost a mirror image of UKIP's 2015 map (constituency correlation r = 0.86) — a re-emergence and consolidation more than an unprecedented new tide.3

That vehicle has since gone further. Through 2025–26 Reform led national voting-intention polls, peaking around 34% in September 2025 before settling near 25% in spring 2026, with Labour and the Conservatives tied well behind.6 Those are volatile poll estimates, not a recorded result — but the direction is clear, and the combined Conservative-plus-Labour share at the 2024 election, 57.4%, was already a record low.8

What we don't know

The two value scales are robust but partial. They were designed around economics, order and personal morality — and capture immigration and gender/trans debates only weakly, the very issues that animate today's culture-war politics.7 So “more socially liberal on the scale” does not mean more liberal on every flashpoint. Vote intention is also not the same as a settled value, and current polls are volatile: Ipsos changed its method in mid-2025 (now prompting for Reform), so comparisons across that break need care.6 Averages can also hide a more mobilised flank — see the caveat below. None of this establishes cause.

Where it's contested

Is Britain actually pulling apart?

Whether the country is “polarising” is genuinely disputed among credible researchers — partly because the word hides several different things. This is an empirical dispute, and the evidence points in both directions.

The case: yes, it's fragmenting

Party support has splintered: the two main parties' combined share hit a record low, and Reform and the Greens are rising at opposite poles.8 “Affective” polarisation around Brexit identities is real, the share of Britons who say the country feels divided has reached a record 84%, and analysts find some social and geographic divides hardening rather than healing.43

Empirical dispute
The counter-case: it's overstated

Classic issue polarisation — opinions spreading apart — is weak: on most social questions Britain is converging on more liberal answers.7 And 62% of the public think politicians invent or exaggerate culture wars as a tactic, up from 44% in 2020 — suggesting much of the “war” is elite-driven rather than a deep split in values.4

Empirical dispute

A reasonable reading: identity-based and party-system fragmentation are real and growing; a wholesale spreading of underlying attitudes is not. Both can be true at once.

Caveat — the strongest objection. An average moving left is consistent with a louder, more mobilised right flank. NatCen finds Reform's recent gains were largest among the most right-wing third of voters and among the over-55s.1 So “the typical Briton moved left and liberal” and “the right got bigger and louder at the ballot box” are both true. The intuition that the country feels more right-wing captures the second; the value scales capture the first.
