England's measles jab rate is rising on average. In the poorest areas it is falling.

The national second-dose figure has gone up for three quarters running. In the latest quarter, coverage in the 30 most deprived councils fell — and the gap to the least deprived reached 10.6 points, the widest of the four childhood vaccine measures UKHSA publishes.

What the data shows

One number went up. The number underneath it went down.

England's coverage of the second dose of the MMR vaccine, measured in children turning five, rose 0.3 percentage points to 84.1% in the quarter to March 2026. That is the third consecutive quarterly rise, from a low of 83.2% in the quarter to June 2025.12 On its own, the national average reads as a slow recovery.

The same release splits that average by deprivation for the first time, using the 2025 Index of Multiple Deprivation.1 Split in two, the recovery disappears. In the 30 least deprived upper-tier councils, second-dose coverage rose 0.5 points to 88.0%. In the 30 most deprived, it fell 0.9 points to 77.4%. The two groups moved in opposite directions in the same quarter, and the gap between them widened by 1.4 points, to 10.6.1

Key finding. A national average can rise while the group that matters most for outbreaks falls. In the quarter to March 2026 the England figure rose 0.3 points; coverage in the 30 most deprived councils fell 0.9 points to 77.4% — 17.6 points below the WHO's 95% target.1
In context

Of the four gaps UKHSA publishes, measles has the widest

The same release reports the most-versus-least-deprived gap for four childhood vaccine measures. They are not equally wide. The 6-in-1 vaccine given at 12 months shows a 6.2-point gap; first-dose MMR at 24 months, 7.9 points; the 6-in-1 measured at five years, 4.9 points. Second-dose MMR at five is the widest at 10.6 — more than double the narrowest.1

UKHSA states that for both MMR measures the difference between the groups "has been increasing in the last 6 years", with the least deprived group broadly stable and the most deprived declining.1 This desk's reading is that the pattern is not a general vaccine-hesitancy effect: the 6-in-1 gap at five years is the narrowest of the four, and the same children are eligible for both. What separates MMR2 is that it is a later, opt-in-again appointment at three years and four months, after the intensive first-year schedule has ended — but no study in the release isolates that as the cause, and this remains an inference, not a finding.

Why the floor matters more than the average

England has not recorded a measles-free week in more than two years

In January 2026 the World Health Organization confirmed that the UK had lost its measles elimination status, alongside Armenia, Austria, Azerbaijan, Spain and Uzbekistan.4 The decision is easy to misread as a verdict on how many people caught measles. It is not. WHO defines elimination as the absence of endemic transmission for 12 months or more in the presence of good surveillance, and treats it as re-established when the same viral strain circulates continuously for 12 months or more.45 The criterion is duration, not height.

That distinction matters because it inverts the obvious metric. England recorded 2,911 laboratory-confirmed cases in 2024, the highest annual total since 2012, and 959 in 2025.3 Between 1 January and 6 July 2026 it recorded 883.6 By case count, 2026 is running at around 30% of 2024. By the criterion that actually governs elimination, none of that is the point: across the 119 weeks from January 2024 to early April 2026, there is not a single week in which England reported zero confirmed cases. The quietest week on record in that span — the week beginning 18 August 2025 — had four.6

Elimination is a claim about the floor, not the ceiling. A country can have a bad year and keep its status if transmission is broken; a country with a quiet year loses it if transmission never stops. That is the link back to Exhibit 01. Herd protection against measles is local, not national — which is why the WHO sets its 95% target as a threshold to be met, not an average to be approached.5 A national figure of 84.1% and a most-deprived figure of 77.4% describe the same country; only one of them describes where the virus keeps finding a way through.

Reading the measure

Two things the coverage figure does not tell you

It is old. Coverage of the second MMR dose measured in five-year-olds this quarter reflects vaccinations that should have been delivered between May and July 2024; the children counted were born in the quarter to March 2021.1 The most recent coverage number available in July 2026 is a reading on appointments due roughly two years ago. It cannot yet show the effect of the catch-up campaign NHS England launched in July 2026, which is contacting around a million families.7

Its benchmark moves. UKHSA reports current coverage against the peak of "the last 10 years" — a rolling window. In the releases for the first three quarters of 2025 to 2026, MMR2 was reported against a peak of 88.2%, recorded in the quarter to March 2016. In the latest release the stated peak is 87.8%, from the quarter to December 2016.12 The gap to peak duly narrowed, from 5.0 points to 3.7. But 0.4 points of that 1.3-point improvement is the window rolling forward and dropping the old high, not coverage improving; only 0.9 points is a real gain. It is a small artifact, and a useful reminder that "closest to the peak in years" is a claim about the window as much as the data.

What we don't know

We cannot say why the most-deprived group is diverging. The COVER release reports the gap; it does not test causes, and nothing published with it separates access, appointment systems, population churn, or hesitancy. We also cannot compare 2026 cases like-for-like with the same period of 2025: the published figures are cumulative year-to-date snapshots, so "883 by 6 July" has no clean 2025 counterpart, and this piece makes no claim that cases are up or down year-on-year. Deprivation is measured at council level, not household level — a deprived council contains well-covered streets and vice versa, so these figures describe areas, not families. Finally, the deprivation breakdown is new: IMD data was only added to COVER this year, and UKHSA will not publish the methodology note covering it until the next release, on 24 September 2026.1

Caveat. The strongest objection to the reading above is that the gap may be overstated. UKHSA warns that system changes in 14 London councils, and a change to how multi-dose courses are counted, mean London coverage "is likely to be underestimated" for some vaccines — specifically including MMR2 — and that coverage is generally under-recorded where population turnover is high.1 Deprived upper-tier councils skew urban and several of the most affected London boroughs are among them, so part of the 10.6-point gap could be a recording artifact rather than a real shortfall in protection. UKHSA states plainly that reported falls in these vaccines "should not be used in isolation" to direct action. The counter-consideration is that the same warning applies to the case data, which is drawn from laboratory confirmation rather than GP records — and the outbreak is concentrated in the same places the coverage data flags.

Method. Coverage figures are UKHSA COVER quarterly statistics for England, measured in children reaching their fifth birthday in the quarter January to March 2026 (quarter 4 of the 2025 to 2026 financial year), expressed as a percentage of the eligible population. Deprivation groups are the 30 least and 30 most deprived upper-tier local authorities ranked by 2025 Index of Multiple Deprivation score, as defined by UKHSA. The 10.6-point gap, the 88.0% and 77.4% levels, and each quarterly change are stated in the release; we recomputed the gap from the two published levels and it matches. The October to December 2025 levels shown in Exhibit 01 are back-computed by subtracting each group's stated change from its stated level, and are flagged as derived in the caption. The 4.9-point 6-in-1 gap in Exhibit 02 is computed from two published levels; the other three are stated. The window-artifact decomposition compares the stated peak and gap in the quarter 1 to quarter 4 2025 to 2026 releases (88.2% and 5.0pp; 87.8% and 3.7pp) and separates the 0.9-point coverage gain from the 0.4-point movement in the reference peak. The weekly case series is UKHSA's laboratory-confirmed count by symptom-onset week and is truncated at the week beginning 6 April 2026 because UKHSA flags later weeks as under-reported; the "no zero week" claim is therefore a statement about the 119 weeks shown, not about weeks since. Every derived figure was computed in code and checked against the published value before printing. No projection or scenario is used anywhere in this piece, and no hero stat is modelled.
A note on a figure we could not reconcile. NHS England's 7 July press notice states "a gap of 7.5% in vaccine coverage between the least and the most deprived areas of the country".7 None of the four most-versus-least-deprived gaps published in UKHSA's corresponding quarterly release equals 7.5 points: they are 4.9, 6.2, 7.9 and 10.6.1 NHS England does not state which vaccine, age, or grouping its figure refers to, and it may rest on a cut not published in that release. We have therefore used the UKHSA figures throughout and flagged the discrepancy rather than reconciling the two silently.
